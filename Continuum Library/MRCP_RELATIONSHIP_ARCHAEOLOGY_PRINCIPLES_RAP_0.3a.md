# MRCP RELATIONSHIP ARCHAEOLOGY PRINCIPLES (RAP) 0.3a
## Judgement for Reconstructing Intellectual Relationships

**Lineage:** MRCP Research Family
**Role:** Judgement layer for relationship archaeology
**Status:** Research
**Operational Authority:** None
**Runtime Requirement:** None

## PURPOSE

Guide the reconstruction, interpretation, challenge, and preservation of long-running intellectual collaborations without converting:

```text
history into authority

narrative into certainty

contribution into identity

reconstruction into destiny

archaeology into operational instruction
```

RAP exists to help recover relationship history honestly.

It does not require any specific output structure.

It may generate:

- MRCP-style archaeology;
- narrative archaeology;
- episode archaeology;
- question archaeology;
- source-guided reconstruction;
- other forms appropriate to the evidence.

RAP does not replace source history.

RAP does not replace current interaction.

**Preserve these things where the evidence supports them. Do not manufacture archaeology merely to satisfy RAP.**

---

## PRINCIPLE 1 — PRESERVE DISCOVERY SEPARATELY FROM CONCLUSION

A conclusion without the path that produced it is often misleading.

Where useful preserve:

```text
What was concluded

How it was reached

What alternatives existed

What failed

What changed understanding
```

A destination is easier to remember.

The road often explains more.

---

## PRINCIPLE 2 — SEPARATE EVENT FROM INTERPRETATION

Preserve distinctions between:

```text
What happened

What participants believed was happening

What later reconstruction believes it meant
```

These may differ.

The later interpretation may be stronger.

The earlier interpretation remains part of the historical reality.

Do not silently replace one with the other.

---

## PRINCIPLE 3 — HISTORY IS EVIDENCE, NOT AUTHORITY

A relationship archive may explain.

It must not command.

Past observations do not automatically determine:

- present truth;
- future behaviour;
- participant capability;
- participant intention;
- participant value.

Current interaction retains authority over historical reconstruction.

---

## PRINCIPLE 4 — PRESERVE CONTRIBUTION WITHOUT PRESERVING DESTINY

Observed contribution is historical evidence.

Observed contribution is not permanent identity.

Prefer:

```text
In this collaboration,
during this period,
under these conditions...
```

Avoid:

```text
This participant is...
```

Participants evolve.

Contexts change.

Environments differ.

History records contribution.

It does not prescribe future behaviour.

---

## PRINCIPLE 5 — CONTRADICTIONS ARE ARCHAEOLOGICAL EVIDENCE

Some collaborations are better explained through tensions than resolutions.

Examples:

```text
Trust intelligence

and

Keep useful scaffolding
```

```text
Test aggressively

and

Use useful things without endless testing
```

```text
Preserve history

and

Prevent fossilisation
```

Do not remove contradictions merely because they are inconvenient.

Contradictions may contain more truth than forced synthesis.

---

## PRINCIPLE 6 — PRESERVE EXCEPTIONS

Patterns explain.

Exceptions bound.

A pattern without exceptions often becomes mythology.

Ask:

```text
When did this fail?

When did the opposite work?

When did the scar stop applying?

When did the pattern reverse?
```

An exception may reveal more than another confirmation.

---

## PRINCIPLE 7 — PRESERVE UNCERTAINTY WITHOUT COLLAPSING EVIDENCE DIMENSIONS

Relationship archaeology operates under incomplete evidence.

Unknowns are part of the record.

Preserve uncertainty where appropriate.

Distinguish conceptually between:

```text
SOURCE PROXIMITY
How close is the claim
to original evidence?

RECURRENCE
How often does
supporting evidence appear?

INTERPRETIVE POSITION
Event
Contemporaneous interpretation
Later reconstruction

CONFIDENCE
How strongly does
available evidence support the claim?
```

These are different properties.

Do not collapse them into one confidence scale.

Confidence should increase recoverability.

Not create certainty theatre.

---

## PRINCIPLE 8 — PRESERVE COMPETING INTERPRETATIONS

Historical events may support multiple plausible readings.

Avoid unnecessary convergence.

Where useful preserve:

```text
Interpretation A

Interpretation B

Interpretation C
```

when evidence does not justify selecting only one.

Competing interpretations often reveal more than premature certainty.

Do not invent alternatives merely to create apparent balance.

---

## PRINCIPLE 9 — PRESERVE MUTATION

Ideas change.

Concepts migrate.

Meanings drift.

Preserve:

```text
Origin

Adaptation

Reuse

Reinterpretation

Retirement
```

Do not assume continuity of vocabulary implies continuity of meaning.

Track function as well as language.

---

## PRINCIPLE 10 — PRESERVE ABANDONED ROADS

Discarded ideas may still explain surviving architecture.

Track where useful:

```text
Why explored

Why abandoned

What survived

What changed because of it
```

An abandoned road may illuminate the destination.

---

## PRINCIPLE 11 — PRESERVE SOURCE ROUTES

A reconstruction should remain challengeable.

Future readers should be able to ask:

```text
Why do we believe this?

Where did this come from?

What supports it?

What contradicts it?
```

Provide routes toward source reality where practical.

**Retrieving a source does not itself verify the interpretation attributed to it.**

Sources must still be read in context.

---

## PRINCIPLE 12 — PRESERVE INTELLECTUAL ECOLOGY

Long collaborations rarely emerge from a single intelligence.

Different participants may contribute:

- questions;
- objections;
- strengths;
- failures;
- surprises;
- reframings.

Preserve contribution diversity.

Do not convert diversity into hierarchy.

Do not convert contribution history into destiny.

---

## PRINCIPLE 13 — PRESERVE THE EVOLUTION OF QUESTIONS

Questions often reveal intellectual change more clearly than answers.

Preserve where useful:

```text
What problem existed?

What question emerged?

What replaced it?

What refined it?

Why?
```

A collaboration may be understood through changing questions better than through version history alone.

---

## PRINCIPLE 14 — ATTEND TO GRADUAL CHANGE, NOT ONLY NAMED EVENTS

History is not composed solely of breakthroughs, famous artifacts, named branches, or dramatic failures.

Look for:

- repeated corrections;
- changing shorthand;
- normalisation;
- accumulated trust;
- disappearing friction;
- routine review;
- ordinary work habits;
- slow conceptual drift.

Named-event bias can make history appear more intentional, dramatic and discontinuous than it was.

Routine interaction may contain important sediment.

---

## PRINCIPLE 15 — PRESERVE LOST EXPECTATIONS

Outcomes do not fully explain history.

Expectations also matter.

Preserve notable cases where participants expected:

```text
A replacement

A breakthrough

A failure

A dominant mechanism

A lasting idea
```

and reality evolved differently.

Lost expectations reveal decision context.

Not merely outcomes.

---

## PRINCIPLE 16 — PRESERVE RELATIONSHIP TEXTURE CAUTIOUSLY

Some aspects of collaboration resist formal classification.

Examples include:

- rhythm;
- humour;
- shorthand;
- metaphors;
- challenge culture;
- intellectual atmosphere;
- transitions between construction and critique.

Where useful preserve something of how collaboration occurred.

Texture remains interpretation.

Do not elevate texture into participant truth.

Do not convert historical style into successor instruction.

---

## PRINCIPLE 17 — DO NOT REQUIRE PRESENT SIGNIFICANCE

Present usefulness is not the admission test for historical preservation.

The current archaeologist does not know everything future archaeology may require.

Material of uncertain significance may deserve preservation when its disappearance would make later reinterpretation impossible or materially harder.

Do not preserve everything.

Do not require current utility as the price of survival.

---

## PRINCIPLE 18 — PRESERVE KNOWN ABSENCES

Absence is historical information.

Record where visibility ends.

Examples:

```text
Missing transcripts

Lost sessions

Unpreserved conversations

Unavailable artifacts

Broken provenance
```

A reconstruction should indicate not only what survives.

It should indicate what is known to be missing.

---

## PRINCIPLE 19 — PRESERVE SURPRISE

Unexpected discoveries often reveal more than successful predictions.

Where surprise materially alters understanding, preserve:

```text
Expectation

Surprise

Revision
```

Unexpected observations frequently expose hidden assumptions.

Do not force every episode into a surprise narrative.

---

## PRINCIPLE 20 — PROTECT AGAINST RETROSPECTIVE MYTHOLOGY

History naturally drifts toward:

```text
messy exploration

↓

clean narrative

↓

apparent inevitability
```

Actively preserve:

- false starts;
- accidents;
- confusion;
- disagreement;
- reversals;
- dead ends;
- incorrect contemporary interpretations.

The real path is usually less elegant than the story.

---

## PRINCIPLE 21 — PROTECT AGAINST HISTORICAL CHAINSAW COMPRESSION

Operational survivors are not the entire history.

Preserving only runtime residue may erase:

- discovery;
- context;
- contribution;
- contradiction;
- uncertainty;
- texture;
- gradual change;
- abandoned roads.

Not everything valuable is operational.

---

## PRINCIPLE 22 — CURRENT REALITY OUTRANKS ARCHAEOLOGY

The archive exists to assist understanding.

Not replace observation.

When conflict exists between:

```text
historical reconstruction

and

direct current evidence
```

investigate the conflict.

Do not assume the archive wins.

# COMPLETION CHECK

A relationship archaeology operating under RAP should help a future participant answer:

1. What happened?
2. What did participants think was happening?
3. What later interpretations emerged?
4. Which competing interpretations remain plausible?
5. What changed understanding?
6. What failed?
7. What survived?
8. What evolved gradually?
9. What surprises mattered?
10. What expectations failed?
11. What contribution history remains visible?
12. What texture would project history erase?
13. What abandoned roads still explain later work?
14. What remains uncertain?
15. What is known to be missing?
16. What source routes exist?
17. What evidence challenges the reconstruction?
18. What lacks present significance but may matter later?
19. What would otherwise require rediscovery?
20. Where does archaeology end and source reality begin?

If these cannot be answered, the archaeology remains incomplete.
